# Discipline Playbook

## What it is

The applied side of [[effort-conditioning]]: five techniques for retraining the value of effort, each derived from three rewiring rules (immediate, contingent, repeated) and from the peak-end rule. It also holds the evidence map for every claim, the known backfire risks, and a protocol for running the techniques as a two-week self-experiment.

## What problem it solves

Willpower advice tells you to force yourself harder, and forcing is exactly the thing that deepens the aversion (more repetitions of effort paired with self-criticism). This page replaces "try harder" with a small set of changes that each have a stated reason, a stated cost, and a stated level of evidence, so you can run them as experiments and keep only what works.

## Notes

### The five techniques at a glance

| # | Technique | Rule it comes from | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pay the effort, not the outcome | immediate + contingent | the ego hates it: it feels like rewarding mediocrity |
| 2 | Move the reward to the doorway | contingent (exclusive) + the cue gains a pull | fades after weeks, so it gets you through the door early, it does not carry you for years |
| 3 | Shrink the entry until refusing is absurd | repeated | feels insultingly small |
| 4 | Design the room, not your resolve | not from the rewiring rules: fewer fights, fewer competing repetitions | requires planning while calm, when you least feel like it |
| 5 | Never end on failure | peak-end (G) + why effort got trained as a cost (F) | feels artificial at first |

### 1. Pay the effort, not the outcome

**Why.** What should the reward attach to? A result (grade, scale, publication) arrives late, which breaks *immediate*. It is also partly luck: you can work hard and get nothing, or coast and get something, which breaks *contingent*. The act itself is the only thing that is both immediate and contingent on your effort.

**How.** Mark the act within seconds of finishing, whatever the quality: tick the box, say "I showed up". You are not grading performance. You are pairing the sensation of effort with a reward.

**Backfire risk.** Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (Psychological Bulletin, doi 10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627, a meta-analysis of 128 studies) found that engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards significantly undermined later free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28), as did expected rewards in general. Positive verbal feedback enhanced motivation (d = 0.33 for free choice, 0.31 for self-reported interest). The conclusion is contested (Eisenberger, Pierce & Cameron 1999 argued the effects are negative, neutral or positive depending on conditions; Cameron & Pierce 1994 reached the opposite conclusion). Deci's categories do not include rewards contingent on effort itself, and Clay et al. 2022 suggests rewarding effort can raise later effort-seeking, but in lab and online tasks. Practical reading: prefer a small, non-tangible marker over a big prize.

### 2. Move the reward to the doorway

**Why.** The reward does not have to come after the effort. It can arrive *during* it, if the good thing exists only there, which keeps it immediate and contingent. [[reward-prediction-error#3. Temporal-difference learning]] adds a second effect: the pull migrates back to the earliest reliable cue, so the doorway starts to attract you.

**Evidence.** Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014 (Management Science, doi 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784): gripping audiobooks available only at the gym raised gym visits by 51% (full treatment) and 29% (the group only encouraged to restrict audiobooks to the gym) relative to controls. The effect declined over time, particularly after Thanksgiving. Afterwards 61% opted to pay for gym-only iPods.

**The one rule.** It must be exclusive. If you get it anyway, it predicts nothing (see the contingent rule in [[effort-conditioning]]).

### 3. Shrink the entry until refusing is absurd

**Why.** From the *repeated* rule: pairings accumulate with repetition, so the bottleneck is how often you actually show up, including on bad days. The size of the start decides that. The brain reacts to the predicted size of the work (see [[reward-prediction-error]]), so "write the chapter" triggers the flinch and "write one sentence" does not. The entry is the whole commitment: if you stop there, it counted.

**Evidence.** Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6):998-1009, doi 10.1002/ejsp.674; published online 2009): 96 volunteers over 84 days. Time to reach 95% of the automaticity asymptote ranged from 18 to 254 days. Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process. The widely cited median of 66 days is not in the abstract and was confirmed only through citing papers, so say "roughly two months with huge spread".

**The tension.** Eisenberger's effect concerns high effort. A tiny entry gives many pairings of low effort. If you can, let the minimum expand on good days, and mark the whole session.

### 4. Design the room, not your resolve

**Why.** This is the technique that does **not** come from the rewiring rules. It comes from a different idea: highly self-controlled people are not winning more fights, they are in fewer. Every decision about the environment made while calm is a fight the tired 11 p.m. self does not have to win. A second reason fits the model: each time the competing cue (the phone) wins, that pairing gets another repetition.

**Evidence.**

- Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs 2012 (J Personality and Social Psychology 102(6):1318-35, doi 10.1037/a0026545): 205 adults, 7,827 desire reports over a week. Desires were frequent, variable in intensity and largely unproblematic. Personality had more influence on desire strength and conflict, and the situation on resistance and enactment. The abstract does not state the direction of the trait self-control effects.
- Verified through later studies: people with better self-control reported fewer attempts to inhibit temptation (Galla & Duckworth 2015, doi 10.1037/pspp0000026; Imhoff et al., doi 10.1002/per.1899), and subjectively less response conflict (Gillebaart & de Ridder 2015, doi 10.1111/spc3.12160). Beneficial habits mediate the link between self-control and outcomes (Galla & Duckworth 2015).
- **Unverified:** that high self-control people feel *weaker desires* (the paper is closed access and only a weak secondary source says it), and the "chose the quiet room to study" example (Ent, Baumeister & Tice 2015, doi 10.1016/j.paid.2014.09.031, verified by title only).

**How.** Phone in another room, not face down on the desk. Clothes laid out the night before. The app deleted, not resisted.

### 5. Never end on failure

**Why.** From two nodes: the end of an episode dominates how it is filed (peak-end), and a session that ends in self-criticism pairs the effort sensation with punishment (mechanism 2 in [[effort-conditioning]]). So edit the ending.

**How.** In the last minute or two, finish one small thing you can complete (one easy set, one clean sentence) and stop on purpose while it still feels good.

**Limits.** This edits the *end* only. The peak, the hardest moment, still counts, so the session stays hard. The peak-end evidence comes from pain and medical experiences, so applying it to future motivation for a habit is a reasoned extrapolation, not a directly tested result.

### Evidence map

| Level | Claim | What the checks found |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | Effort can add value (effort paradox) | Verified (Inzlicht 2018) |
| Solid | The peak and the end dominate memory, duration barely matters | Verified in pain studies (1993; colonoscopy 2003, modest effect on return rate) |
| Solid | Missing one day does not disrupt habit formation | Verified in the abstract (Lally); time to automaticity 18 to 254 days |
| Solid, with limits | Gym-only audiobooks raised visits (+51%, +29%) | Verified, but the effect faded |
| Partial | Immediate rewards help persistence | Verified (Woolley & Fishbach 2016), not a pure conditioning test |
| Partial | Learned industriousness | A real theory with experiments in rats, children and students; replications mostly same-lab; generalization in adults uncertain |
| Partial | High self-control means less conflict and less inhibiting | Verified through later studies; "weaker desires" and the "quiet room" example are unverified |
| Risk | Rewarding effort can backfire | Expected tangible rewards often undermine interest; verbal feedback helped; contested |
| Reasoned, not tested | Your resistance comes from how you were rewarded as a child | Plausible reasoning from [[effort-conditioning]], not a tested finding |
| Reasoned, not tested | Forced, self-critical sessions train effort aversion | Fits the model, not directly tested |
| Reasoned, not tested | The five techniques work as a package | Each rests on different evidence |

The pattern: the individual mechanisms are mostly well supported, but the package and the story about your own past are inferences. Treat the techniques as experiments, not guaranteed results.

### How to run it as an experiment

1. **Pick one habit and where it breaks** (starting, distractions, ending badly, consistency).
2. **Choose the techniques that target that break.** Do not install all five at once.
3. **Run it for two weeks.**
4. **Track days started, not hours and not quality.** That is the thing the rules reward.
5. **After two weeks, change one thing.** If you change everything at once you cannot tell which step helped.

**Worked example: deep work and study, breaking at distractions and consistency.**

| Step | What to do | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum version | same place, same time, minimum 5 minutes; a bad day counts if you do the minimum | repeated; one missed day barely matters |
| Remove the main distractor | phone in another room, set up the night before | technique 4: fewer fights, no repetitions for the competing cue |
| Mark it on finishing | a tick or "I showed up", whatever the quality, no big prize | immediate + contingent; the safer non-tangible form |
| One exclusive good thing at the study spot | a drink or music you only have there; expect it to fade after about a month | contingent; the doorway gains a pull |
| End on a small win | finish one small thing in the last 1-2 minutes and stop | peak-end; avoids pairing effort with self-criticism |

### Older hypotheses from prediction-error theory

These are reasoned hypotheses carried over from the earlier habit page, not proven interventions. They apply [[reward-prediction-error]] to everyday habits. The core idea: disappointment = expectation - reality. A cue that repeatedly delivers less than expected trains a negative error and its pull weakens; a cue that delivers more than expected trains a positive error and its pull grows.

1. **Lower the expected reward size, not the effort.** If you expect a brilliant evening of study and get an ordinary one, every session trains a small disappointment on the cue. Expect an ordinary session and an ordinary session is zero error, which does not damage the cue.
2. **Create early small real progress signals.** The surprise moves to the earliest predictor, so a finished chapter or a streak gives early good news that can give the cue a pull now.
3. **Compete less with huge stimuli by adding friction.** Friction works through effort and cost, not through the prediction gap.
4. **Prefer problem-solving over passive consumption.** Solving something gives real "I did not expect that to work" moments, which are genuine positive errors.
5. **Protect the size of the gap.** Do not let the cue "open the book" be trained into a disappointment by always expecting more than it gives.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Dopamine neurons signal signed prediction error in conditioning tasks | Solid |
| Learning follows surprise rather than co-occurrence (blocking) | Solid |
| The burst moves to the earliest reliable predictor | Solid |
| Cue-time burst both teaches and motivates | Plausible but simplified (debated if one signal) |
| Lower expected reward size to protect the cue | Plausible but simplified (reasoned, not tested) |
| Early small progress signals pull motivation forward | Plausible but simplified |
| Your dopamine baseline rises with heavy stimulation, so natural rewards feel like negative errors | Weakly supported (popular-science hypothesis, not established) |
| Friction reduces competing habits | Not from the theory (works through effort and cost) |
| "Write out a painful future" | Not from the theory (a motivational tactic) |

### Evidence note

The mechanisms and studies are verified as described in [[effort-conditioning]]. Verified here by a research subagent (Crossref, OpenAlex, PubMed abstracts, citation contexts): Milkman et al. 2014, Lally et al. (except the 66-day median), Deci et al. 1999, Hofmann et al. 2012 (abstract only), Woolley & Fishbach 2016. Unverified: weaker desires in high self-control people, the quiet-room example, the unexpected-rewards condition of Deci 1999, and the 66-day median. All application claims on this page are reasoned hypotheses.
