# RPE and Habit Change

## What it is

Reasoned hypotheses for applying reward prediction error theory to self-learning and habits. These are inferences from the theory, not proven interventions.

## What problem it solves

Why does "open the book" lose to "scroll the phone"? The theory suggests that a cue gets its pull from the gap between what it promises and what it delivers. Thinking in terms of that gap gives concrete things to adjust.

## Notes

### The core idea

Disappointment = expectation − reality. A cue that repeatedly delivers less than expected trains a negative error, and its pull weakens (the lemon tree). A cue that delivers more than expected trains a positive error, and its pull grows.

### Five hypotheses

1. **Lower the expected reward size, not the effort.** If you expect a brilliant evening of study and get an ordinary one, every session trains a small disappointment on the cue. Expect an ordinary session and an ordinary session is zero error, which does not damage the cue.
2. **Create early small real progress signals.** The surprise moves to the earliest predictor (see [[temporal-difference-learning]]), so a finished chapter or a streak gives early good news that can give the cue a pull now instead of waiting for a distant payoff.
3. **Compete less with huge stimuli by adding friction.** A phone is a very large, very reliable cue. Friction (phone in another room) works through effort and cost, not through the prediction gap.
4. **Prefer problem-solving over passive consumption.** Solving something gives real "I did not expect that to work" moments, which are genuine positive errors. Passive consumption can only match expectation.
5. **Protect the size of the gap.** Do not let the cue "open the book" become trained into a disappointment by always expecting more than it gives.

### Evidence status

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Dopamine neurons signal signed prediction error in conditioning tasks | Solid |
| Learning follows surprise rather than co-occurrence (blocking) | Solid |
| The burst moves to the earliest reliable predictor | Solid |
| Cue-time burst both teaches and motivates | Plausible but simplified (debated if one signal) |
| Lower expected reward size to protect the cue | Plausible but simplified (reasoned, not tested) |
| Early small progress signals pull motivation forward | Plausible but simplified |
| Your dopamine baseline rises with heavy stimulation, so natural rewards feel like negative errors | Weakly supported (popular-science hypothesis, not established) |
| Friction reduces competing habits | Not from the theory (works through effort and cost) |
| "Write out a painful future" | Not from the theory (a motivational tactic, not derived from RPE) |

### Evidence note

The theory-side claims are verified against the sources in [[reward-prediction-error]]. All application claims are reasoned hypotheses. The "baseline rises" idea comes from a popular video that presented it as fact, and it is not established (see [[rpe-open-questions]]).
