# Dopamine: Wanting vs Liking

## What it is

Dopamine is not the "happiness chemical". In the prediction-error picture it signals surprise and drives the pull toward a reward, which is different from the pleasure of having it. Wanting is the pull toward something. Liking is the pleasure while it happens.

## What problem it solves

The popular story "dopamine = pleasure" cannot explain a basic observation: a fully predicted reward produces no dopamine burst, yet it is still enjoyed. Separating wanting from liking explains why you can strongly want something you barely enjoy.

## Notes

### The puzzle

After learning, the dopamine burst moves from the reward to the cue (see [[reward-prediction-error]] and [[temporal-difference-learning]]). If dopamine were pleasure, a fully predicted meal should feel like nothing. It does not. So the burst is not the pleasure itself. It marks the moment of surprise and anticipation.

### Wanting vs liking

| | Wanting | Liking |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | pull toward something | pleasure while it happens |
| Everyday example | reaching for the phone | enjoying a good meal |
| Linked to dopamine | closely, as a push toward cues | less clearly (this split is debated, see the evidence note) |

Scrolling a phone is the standard case: high wanting (you keep reaching for it), low liking (you often do not enjoy it much). The cue pulls, and the pull is trained by past errors.

### How it connects to the error signal

The burst at a cue is both a lesson and a push. The push size depends on how good the cue looks, which is set by past prediction errors. After a lemon from a tree, tomorrow that tree pulls less. Whether the push and the teaching signal are the same signal is debated.

### Evidence note

From memory and unverified here: animal research on a wanting/liking split exists and is associated with Kent Berridge. The distinction itself is debated. Verified: the canonical dopamine response patterns (Schultz 1998).
